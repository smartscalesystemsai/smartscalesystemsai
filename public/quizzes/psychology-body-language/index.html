Psychology Tricks & Body Language

15 questions with answers and explanations · free to read and reuse

Body language content online is mostly overclaimed. Single cues are read as proof of deception or attraction when the evidence supports nothing so specific.

The overclaiming follows a pattern: one cue gets treated as a reliable tell for lying, attraction, or hidden intent. Studies of deception detection consistently find people perform barely above chance, and that the cues popularly believed to indicate lying mostly do not.

Where a cue is genuinely informative, it is informative as context rather than proof. Feet angled toward an exit may mean someone wants to leave, or that the chair is uncomfortable, or nothing at all. The questions here are worded to reflect that, which is why several correct answers include a hedge — the hedge is the accurate part.

The cognitive-bias questions rest on firmer ground. Anchoring, the illusory truth effect, the frequency illusion and the peak-end rule are well replicated. They are also more useful than any body-language tell, because they describe things happening in your own head that you can notice and account for — such as recognizing when a familiar-sounding claim feels true mainly because you have heard it several times.

Every question below shows all four options, the keyed answer, and the one-line explanation. If you want to turn these into video, the OmniFrame Quiz Studio is free and this pack is built in.

All 15 questions

Question 1

A person says they are fine, but their feet point toward the exit. What might that signal?

  • Proof of deception
  • Certain anger
  • A medical diagnosis
  • Possible desire to leave, not proof  ← answer

Why: Foot direction can be a context clue, but a single body-language cue cannot establish intent.

Question 2

Why does a fake countdown make people click faster?

  • Scarcity pressure  ← answer
  • Better arithmetic
  • Improved memory
  • Reduced screen glare

Why: Urgency reduces careful decision-making.

Question 3

What bias makes expensive items feel better before you try them?

  • Sunk-cost fallacy
  • Price-placebo effect  ← answer
  • Bystander effect
  • Zeigarnik effect

Why: Expectations can change perceived value.

Question 4

Why does repeating a statement make it feel truer?

  • Dunning-Kruger effect
  • Halo effect
  • Illusory truth effect  ← answer
  • Choice overload

Why: Familiarity can be mistaken for accuracy.

Question 5

What makes a memory feel vivid but still unreliable?

  • Permanent recording
  • Exact playback
  • Photographic storage
  • Reconstruction  ← answer

Why: Memory rebuilds details and can add false confidence.

Question 6

What is it called when you notice something more after learning about it?

  • Frequency illusion  ← answer
  • Anchoring
  • Recency bias
  • Confirmation bias

Why: Your attention makes the thing seem newly common.

Question 7

What body-language trick often builds rapport?

  • Speaking louder
  • Subtle mirroring  ← answer
  • Avoiding all eye contact
  • Crossing the arms

Why: People tend to feel comfort when rhythms and posture gently match.

Question 8

Why do cliffhanger questions hold attention?

  • Closure is already complete
  • The ending is irrelevant
  • Open loop  ← answer
  • Attention ignores missing information

Why: The brain wants closure for incomplete information.

Question 9

What bias makes recent events feel more important?

  • Anchoring
  • Availability from distant events
  • Sunk-cost bias
  • Recency bias  ← answer

Why: Recent information is easier to recall and can feel overweighted.

Question 10

What makes a choice feel easier: 2 options or 12?

  • 12 options
  • Both are always equal
  • Choice count never matters
  • 2 options  ← answer

Why: Too many options can create decision fatigue.

Question 11

What is confirmation bias?

  • Seeking evidence that agrees with you  ← answer
  • Rejecting supporting evidence
  • Forgetting every prior belief
  • Choosing evidence randomly

Why: People often favor information that supports existing beliefs.

Question 12

A classic Duchenne smile involves which area besides the mouth?

  • Shoulder movement
  • Muscles around the eyes  ← answer
  • Foot tapping
  • A louder voice

Why: A Duchenne smile includes contraction around the eyes as well as lifting at the mouth.

Question 13

Why do countdowns increase action?

  • Reduced relevance
  • Perfect certainty
  • Urgency  ← answer
  • More available time

Why: Time limits reduce hesitation.

Question 14

What is anchoring?

  • Last number influence
  • Random-number immunity
  • Ignoring all starting values
  • First number influence  ← answer

Why: The first value seen can shape later judgments.

Question 15

Why do people remember endings strongly?

  • Peak-end effect  ← answer
  • Primacy effect
  • Confirmation bias
  • Mere-exposure effect

Why: Memory often emphasizes intense moments and endings.
