Free quiz questions, with the reasoning

391 questions across 19 packs

Every question here shows its answer and a one-line explanation of why that answer is right. Nothing is gated, nothing needs an account, and you can reuse any of it without asking.

The packs split roughly into two kinds. The reasoning sets — deceptive maths, lateral thinking, logic puzzles — are built around a specific wrong answer that feels correct, and the explanation names that trap rather than merely confirming the key. The knowledge sets test things you have probably encountered without having a word for.

If you are interested in how they are constructed, we wrote up the working rules in what makes a quiz question hard. To turn any of these into video, the OmniFrame Quiz Studio is free and has every pack built in.

How to use these

Nothing here is gated and no attribution is required. Use them in a classroom, a pub quiz, a training session, a video, or a family argument. The answers are shown inline rather than hidden behind a click, because this is meant to work as a reference — if you are building a round, you need to see the answer while you choose.

If you are running a live quiz, a practical tip: read the four options aloud in the order shown. The correct answer is spread roughly evenly across all four positions across the whole set, so reading them in order does not leak anything. That distribution is maintained deliberately, because writers unconsciously favor certain slots and a regular player will start reading your habits rather than solving your questions.

What we mean by a good question

The bar these were written to is narrow. Too easy and nobody feels anything when the answer lands. Too obscure and the player never had a chance, which reads as unfair rather than clever. The questions worth keeping sit between those failures — close enough that the answer feels retrievable, far enough that most people do not get there.

Two rules do most of the work. First, the intuitive answer should be present as an option, and it should be wrong: the whole effect depends on the player reaching for something confidently and finding out it was the trap. Second, the explanation should name that trap rather than merely confirming the key. "If the ball is 5 cents, the bat is $1.05" is true and useless; "the tempting 10 cents makes the total $1.20" tells the person who got it wrong precisely where they went wrong.

Across the 391 questions here, 388 have an explanation of exactly one sentence, with a median of nine words. The three exceptions are multi-step arithmetic shortcuts, where the method genuinely has two steps. That is not a style preference: a question needing a paragraph to explain was never fair to begin with.
